Can we put a number on "weird"?

Borders should follow geography, so a constituency ought to be roughly convex. Wrap the tightest convex polygon (its convex hull) around it and see how much of that polygon it fills.

About 47% of the hull is empty space: the long northern arms and the narrow neck to the south leave large gaps. A perfectly convex shape would score 100%.

How does 53% compare?

  • K.R. Pura scores 53%: second lowest of Bengaluru’s 28 assembly constituencies. Only Rajarajeshwarinagar (51%) is lower. The Bengaluru median is 70%.
  • Forbes India (2019) ran the same test on Lok Sabha seats: Kaliabor (Assam) 39%, lowest in the country; Bangalore North 51%, fourth lowest; national average 72%.

A good start, but a weak metric

  • Rivers, coastlines and state borders also force ugly shapes.
  • A skilled map-drawer can hit a goal with perfectly tidy-looking districts.
  • A shape score describes one map. It cannot tell us what a fair process would have produced instead.

To answer that, we need a precise model of the maps themselves. That is where graph theory comes in.

Gerrymandering

The practice of (re)drawing district boundaries so that one party or group gets more seats than its votes would normally earn. The name comes from Governor Elbridge Gerry, whose 1812 Massachusetts map produced a rather unusual salamander-shaped district.

“The Gerry-Mander”, Boston Gazette, 1812

AB709-istan: state with 27 blocks

Picture a tiny state made of 27 equal voting blocks in a 3 by 9 layout. Each block leans towards Party A or Party B: 15 lean A and 12 lean B, so A has about 56% of the vote.

The state elects 3 representatives. We must cut it into 3 districts of 9 blocks each, every district in one connected piece.

Each square is a voting block, coloured by the party it leans to

Both maps are connected and equal in size. In Map 2, A's voters are packed into one district (8 of 9 lean A) and cracked across the other two (4 and 3 of 9), so A loses both despite having 56% of the vote.

From a map to a graph

Replace each block by a vertex, and join two vertices by an edge when their blocks share a side. This gives a graph \(G=(V,E)\) with \(|V|=27\), \(|E|=42\). Adjacency is the only geometry we keep.

Districts and their boundaries

A map with 3 districts is a partition \(V=D_1\cup D_2\cup D_3\) into connected pieces of 9 vertices each. Its boundary is the set of edges whose endpoints lie in different districts, forming a cutset.

Map 1: boundary of 6 edges
Map 2: boundary of 12 edges

Which map looks more "compact"?

A compact district is tidy and round; a sprawling one touches many neighbours. In graph terms every block has the same area, and a district's "perimeter" is the number of its edges that get cut. Sprawl means more cut edges.

Map 1, three 3×3 squares: 6
Map 2: 12
Map 3, three 1×9 strips: 18

Boundary size is our first compactness score: smaller is more compact.

The boundary only sees the outside

Two districts can have equally long boundaries and still differ a lot inside. One could be a solid block. The other could be two blobs joined by a thin chain of blocks, where a single edge (a bridge) holds the district together.

The boundary count cannot see that bottleneck. We want a second score that measures how well-knit a district is on the inside.

Spanning trees

A spanning tree of a graph is a connected subgraph with no circuits that touches every vertex. A district of 9 blocks, seen as a graph, has spanning trees with exactly 8 edges. Let \(\tau\) be the number of spanning trees.

Path on 4 vertices: \(\tau=1\)
Square graph: \(\tau=4\)

So \(\tau\) is large exactly when a district is thick and well-connected inside. A 3×3 block has \(\tau=192\); a 1×9 path has \(\tau=1\).

A tree score for the whole map

Is Map 2 suspicious?

We now have numbers for Map 2 (boundary 12, tree score 3,600, one seat for A despite 56% of the vote), but a number needs a reference: is 12 jagged? Is one seat unusual?

So we build the reference: draw many maps of the same state with a party-blind process (call them neutral maps) and see where Map 2 falls among them.

  • Party-blind: the process sees only the graph (which blocks touch, how big each district must be), never which blocks lean A or B. Any tilt in its results comes from geography alone.
  • Compactness built in: maps are drawn with probability proportional to their tree score, so tidy maps come up often and sprawling ones rarely.

Packing and cracking: the tools

Seats go to majorities, so a gerrymander tries to make its own votes count and the other side's votes go to waste. Choosing which blocks to put together, that is, choosing the cut edges, is how.

Map 2 as a graph, districts tinted
  • Packing: pile the other side's voters into a few districts they win by far more than needed. District 2 has 8 A voters; 5 would do, so 3 are wasted.
  • Cracking: split the rest so they fall just short everywhere. A's other 7 voters are split 4 and 3, and lose both districts.
  • Wasted votes (extra votes in a win, plus every vote in a loss): A wastes 10 of its 15 votes, B only 2 of its 12.

Why Map 2 looks suspicious

Map 2: A wins 1 seat of 3 with 56% of the vote
  • Rare outcome: A wins 1 seat in only 2.6% of neutral maps: about 1 draw in 40.
  • Packed and cracked: 8 of 9 lean A in District 2, and 4 and 3 in the others.
  • Somewhat sprawling: boundary 12 against an average of 8.9; tree score 3,600 against typically around 100,000.

Geography alone gives this outcome about once in 40 draws, and Map 2 reaches it with a more sprawling shape than most. The simplest explanation is that its lines were drawn to produce it.

That is evidence, not proof of intent: the test says only that geography is an unlikely explanation. Our state is tiny and our sampler simplified.

North Carolina, 2016: the same test in court

The 2016 congressional map elected 10 Republicans and 3 Democrats. A Duke team led by Jonathan Mattingly drew 24,518 neutral maps of the state and scored each with actual 2016 congressional votes.

What came of it

  • Fewer than 1% of neutral maps gave a result as lopsided as the enacted one. More than 99% gave Democrats at least one more seat.
  • Shape did not give it away: the oddly shaped 2012 map and its tidier 2016 replacement were equally atypical.
  • A federal court struck the map down in 2018. In Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) the Supreme Court held that federal courts cannot decide partisan gerrymandering claims, but ensemble evidence continues to be used in state cases.

Assam, 2023: redrawing the lines

The Election Commission's final delimitation order (11 August 2023) was Assam's first full redraw since 1976. The inputs read like our model:

  • Vertices: villages and wards, kept intact and never split.
  • Population: the 2001 Census, which the Constitution fixes until figures from the first census after 2026 are published. Seats were allocated to the 31 districts (10 in 1976) in three density bands, each with a ±10% population margin.
  • Compactness: "as far as practicable", alongside physical features, administrative boundaries and public convenience.
  • Result: 126 assembly and 14 Lok Sabha seats unchanged in number; ST-reserved assembly seats 16 → 19, SC 8 → 9; Bodoland districts 11 → 15 seats.

Reactions

Concerns raised (opposition parties, Barak Valley groups)
  • Muslim-majority assembly seats fall from 29 to 22, by AIUDF's count.
  • Barak Valley drops from 15 to 13 seats despite population growth, while Bodoland rises from 11 to 15.
  • Congress says the Lok Sabha seats it held (Kaliabor, Nagaon, Barpeta) were targeted.
  • Critics say Muslim-majority seats were drawn with larger populations than Hindu-majority ones.
  • The final order came while petitions were pending in the Supreme Court.
Case made by the ECI and the state government
  • The 2001 Census is what the Constitution requires for now.
  • Villages and wards were left intact; population margins and compactness followed the stated methodology.
  • 1,222 representations were considered and about 45% addressed; 19 seats were renamed.
  • SC and ST seats follow constitutional formulas.
  • The chief minister said seats were decided with people's well-being in mind and that some requests for changes were met; supporters say it protects indigenous communities.

Which of these disagreements are mathematical?

  • Population balance. Do the ±10% margins hold, and are they applied evenly? A count can settle that.
  • Compactness. Boundary and tree scores can measure how sprawling each constituency is.
  • Constraints. Reservation quotas and the Bodoland provisions shape which partitions are allowed.
  • Objective. Which communities seats should protect, and what counts as fair, is a political question.

To apply our test to Assam we would need the final maps and precinct-level vote data, then compare the result with neutral maps built under the same constraints.
